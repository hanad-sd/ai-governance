The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, explained plainly

If you read one document about governing AI, this is a sensible one to start with. It is free, it is practical, and most other frameworks borrow its words.

What it is

The AI Risk Management Framework comes from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. Version 1.0 was released in January 2023. It is not a law. It carries no penalties, and nobody can be fined for ignoring it.

It is a structure any organisation can use to decide how AI is used, tested and watched. A companion document, the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024), applies the same structure to tools like chatbots and writing assistants. NIST says a revised version of the framework is in progress.

Why it matters when it is not law

Its influence comes from other people building on it. Assessors and vendors use its language. Employers name it in job postings. That makes it the closest thing North America has to a shared vocabulary for this work.

A law tells you what is required. This framework answers a different question: how do we run this responsibly, day to day? For most organisations, that is the question in front of them.

How it works: four functions

  • Govern. Decide who is responsible. Who owns each tool, who can stop it, and how decisions are written down. The other three functions depend on this one.
  • Map. Understand the setting before you judge the risk. What is the tool for, who does it affect, what information does it use, and what could go wrong here? Risk belongs to a tool in a particular use, never to the tool alone.
  • Measure. Test against the risks you mapped. Is it accurate? Is it fair? Can it be tricked or misused?
  • Manage. Act on what you found. Decide what to fix, what to accept and what to stop. Keep watching after launch, and have a plan for the day something goes wrong.

What a trustworthy system looks like

The framework lists seven qualities that run through all four functions: valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy-enhanced; and fair, with harmful bias managed. Know this list and you can read almost any governance document and see what it is reaching for.

What a small organisation can take from it

You do not need the whole framework to benefit from it. A team of thirty can take four plain steps from it.

  • Name one person who is responsible for how AI is used. That is Govern.
  • List the AI tools in use and what information goes into each. That is Map.
  • Check the results before anyone relies on them, especially where a person is affected. That is Measure.
  • Agree what happens when a tool gets something wrong, and who decides to stop using it. That is Manage.

Those four steps fit on one page. They are also the outline of the self-check on this site.

Its limits

The framework is voluntary and written in the United States. It does not tell a Canadian organisation what Canadian privacy law requires, and following it does not prove compliance with any law. It gives you shared words and a workable structure. It does not replace advice from your own counsel.
